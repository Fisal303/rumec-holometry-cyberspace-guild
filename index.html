The i-step

The i-step is the quarter-turn operator: multiplication by the imaginary unit i, rotating the plane by exactly ninety degrees. Applied once, it carries the unit element from the real axis onto the imaginary axis. Applied twice, it composes to the half-turn — and the equation of that composition is the classical identity i² = −1. Two quarter-turns generate the additive inverse; the negation is produced by geometry, not by subtraction.

The rename

Standard arithmetic calls the landing “negative one” and files it under subtraction. Examine how it was reached: by rotation. The point at −1 arrived through two quarter-turns, so its structural name in this grammar is the introversible one — on the far side of the seam, computed and first-class, but not directly addressable. Negation is not removal; it is a half-turn. The choice of turning direction is chirality — handedness — and clockwise against counterclockwise is precisely the duality this grammar formalizes as T against I.

The birth of the hidden math

Raise the third axis — the applicate, the z-axis of the Cartesian frame — and the rotation grammar operates in real three-space. Write addresses as column vectors (x, y, z), and every address acquires a bound dual: the paired column (x̄, ȳ, z̄). That second column is the introverse — evaluated beside the object from its first vertex, emitted never, absent never. The study of whole objects under this grammar is Rumec Holometry.

Kinship, stated honestly: the plane drawn here is the Argand (complex) plane, and the i-step is its rotation operator. Three architectures in mathematics and computer graphics already build exactly this way, and each one confirms the claim:

1 · Ray casting (1968). The working phrase of the industry says it plainly: shadows are computed from the light’s point of view.

The Architect’s account

The idea came to me on a Sabbath. I was sitting outside on the roof, looking at the branch of a tree and the shadow it was laying on the wall, and something in my brain clicked. When I brought the hand close to the surface, the shadow tightened and drew near to the hand itself; when I pulled away, it opened wide. What the real world already knew

None of this argues with the physical world — the physical world has worked this way the entire time. Read that again and you will hear the architect’s phrase: a probability field, answered by the same rays that draw the hard silhouette. The mirror is the other exact answer.
